For non-astronomers
===================
To put it broadly, I research galaxies, which are comprised (among many other things) stars and gas. Those stars fuse light elements into heavy elements in their cores, then sometimes explode and scatter those heavy elements throughout and outside the galaxy. Tracing those heavy elements can tell us about the galaxy's history as well as many important characteristics. Specifically, I'm looking at what information we lose when we only observe certain kinds of galaxies instead of all of them, which is impossible.
		
For astronomers
===============
I'm interested in the evolution of the mass-metallicity relation (MZR) through all epochs. In undergrad, I characterized the [LEGA-C MZR](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964...59L/abstract) at z=0.7, with most of the focus on the massive end of the relation. In grad school, I've been a part of the [RUBIES](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A%26A...697A.189D/abstract) collaboration, where I also [measured the MZR](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026ApJ..1005..159L/abstract), this time at z=3-6. We took a different approach in the latter work, introducing a novel semi-Bayesian forward-modeling subtractive process wherein we apply the RUBIES selection function and emission line measurability sampling to a matrix of galaxy models generated with *prospector* to attempt to back out an "intrinsic" MZR and account for survey sampling and SFR bias. I'm currently working to apply that same framework to a host of surveys across cosmic time with the goal of eking out a more robust description of the evolution of the MZR from the epoch of reionization through the current day.
